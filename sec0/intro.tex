\section{Introduction}

Three problems motivate this paper.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Newcomers need guidance in the craft of writing research papers,
and the standard advice is scattered across decades of folklore.
\item When those newcomers turn to large language models for help,
those models generate prose that is bland, verbose, and instantly
recognizable as machine written.
\item Established researchers struggle to find time for research,
since writing papers (and maturing the papers of graduate students)
has grown into a full-time job.
This burden is measurably heavy in software engineering: by one
estimate, one SE paper costs over four times the collaborative effort
of one machine learning paper (4.09 versus 0.86 ICLR
points~\cite{luo25,menzies26se}).
\end{enumerate}

Given this, we take guidance from two sources.
The first is Newell's advice to young scientists, retold by Shaw (who
worked with Newell at CMU) while answering an audience question at her
FSE'26 keynote~\cite{shaw26}:
\begin{quote}
{\em Know what you are good at.
Work from those strengths, not from your weaknesses.
Then point those strengths at whatever your field currently ranks as
its hardest open problems.} --- after Newell~\cite{newell91}
\end{quote}
\note{timm}{wording is our paraphrase; tune it to your memory of the
talk} Applied to paper writing, that advice yields a workflow of small
interim steps, and it gives teams a natural division of labor.
That division is found by asking four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item ``Hello team: what are our recent best papers?''\footnote{Here,
``recent'' means the last five years.
For newcomers to a field, we recommend a small variant of this
question: for the people we can talk to in our local area, what are
their recent best papers?}
\item ``What do those papers reveal, via reverse engineering, as our
unique and greatest strengths?''
\item ``Given those strengths, in what research or application areas
could we work?''
\item ``How could our strengths address the important issues of those
areas?''\footnote{We do not presume to say what ``important issues''
might mean to the reader.
It could be many things: the area of most current commercial interest;
a ``hole'', i.e. an area no one is looking at; a ``mountain'', i.e. an
area everyone is working on; etc. For mountains, two questions can be
insightful: (a)~if everyone is doing something, is everyone doing that
something wrong (in some way)?
(b)~if everyone is doing something, can we optimize that something
(even a little)?}
\end{enumerate}
Hence it addresses our first problem, since a workflow of explicit
steps is teachable in a way that folklore is not.

The second source is an experience base; that is, a database, reverse
engineered from the research corpus, holding (e.g.):
\begin{itemize}
\item state-of-the-art results; \item holes in that state of the art;
\item the experimental standards of particular sub-fields; \item etc.
\end{itemize}
Such a base is not one-size-fits-all.
Using LLMs, hundreds of papers can be downloaded and parsed in
minutes, so a base can be computed fresh for the local task and the
local authors.
Hence our third problem shrinks, since months of manual reading
becomes a background job that runs per project, not once per field.
Further, \textbf{it is now possible to reverse engineer, from a
researcher's own papers, what their particular strengths are and how
those strengths might attack current problems in the field}.
To our knowledge, that inversion was impractical before LLMs.

Several services augment these two ideas:
\begin{itemize}
\item a catalog of anti-patterns seen in LLM-generated text, with
rules for avoiding them; \item a map of the common structures of
research papers; \item automatically generated critiques which, acting
as ICSE's reviewer two, report the weaknesses of the current draft and
propose a revision that avoids at least some of them; \item etc.
\end{itemize}
Hence our second problem also yields, since LLM prose need not be
bland once the machine is told, precisely, what bland looks like.

Before starting, one frequently asked question deserves an answer.
Should we follow Newell's advice at all?
If everyone focuses on their own tricks, each of us risks the guilt of
the hammer that sees only nails.
Our short reply is that strengths choose the tools while the field's
open problems choose the targets; hence the hammer is aimed, not
wandering.
In our experience the method does not rut a career, for two reasons:
its literature step keeps forcing contact with material outside prior
experience, and its experiments force a critical evaluation of the
tool base's own premises.
