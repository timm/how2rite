\section{Reading In}
\label{sec:lit}

A topic chosen is a literature owed.
Our lit-review rules are few enough to fit in a box:
Figure~\ref{fig:prompt} shows the whole prompt, whose first three
lines are machine-read configuration and whose rules drive the
pipeline.

Figure~\ref{fig:lit} shows the rules running on the area that
\S\ref{sec:map} chose.
The venue rule earned its place twice.
A first draft of this section skipped it, and the reading set filled
with general AI blockbusters (a machine learning survey, the GPT-4
report, a metaverse position paper) from venues nowhere near software
engineering.
A second draft filtered venues after a citation-sorted fetch, and that
failed too: of the top 1{,}000 hits, only nine sat in SE venues, since
mega-cited outsiders crowd out the field long before the filter runs.
Hence the venue list joins the query itself, and the human hours are
spent only on papers our field would recognize.

Snowballing then ran from that set, deliberately unfiltered by venue,
since good ancestors and good critics live anywhere: backward
collection found \litRefs{} distinct references (\litClassics{} cited
by two or more of the reading set: the candidate classics); forward
collection found \litCiting{} works already citing the reading set.
\note{timm}{the three flags are paper0-local; a full run would fork
its own workdir and flags.py, per the engine rules}

The process itself yielded findings worth recording.
Full texts are scarce: in this workdir's companion pipeline run, only
22 of 54 chased PDFs (41\%) could be downloaded; abstracts come easier
(here, \litAbs{} of \litKnee).
Google Scholar is closed to scripts; OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, and
arXiv answer machine queries gladly, so the pipeline builds only on
the willing.
An earlier full-text pass showed abstract-only coding missing about
half the flags; hence full text is coded above the knee.
Finally, the own-papers rule paid off: \litOwn{} papers by this team
(Di Nucci on accessibility, Schmid on MLOps, Esposito on AI agents)
already sit in the venue-filtered goal results.
The territory is adjacent to us without yet being occupied by us.

Reading done, the loop starts writing.
Figure~\ref{fig:sample} shows the step-8 draft, whose parts follow
the introduction grammar of \S\ref{sec:write}.
Figure~\ref{fig:samplelit} shows the step-9 literature
characterization in Shaw's scheme~\cite{shaw03}.
The loop generated both for the very topic that \S\ref{sec:map}
nominated; we freeze them here as exhibits rather than polish them
into claims.

\item One word, one meaning; never vary a key term.
\item Kill nominalizations: ``perform an evaluation of'' is
``evaluate''.
\item Numbers carry their arithmetic; claims wait for their
statistics.
\item Fragments never; a short sentence is still a sentence.
\item The abstract carries the paper.
\item The rest is a linter ({\ttfamily etc/style.py}): banned
words, colon rate, paragraph shapes.

\scriptsize
\bnfleaf{problem}
Team formation stalls on its oldest question: what is this team
actually good at, and where does that talent meet the field?
The ICSE'27 call names nine research areas; a six-person team can
staff at most three or four of them well.
Choosing wrongly wastes the scarcest resource: senior attention.
\bnfleaf{quantified\_pain}
Six authors each named their five most-cited papers, all-time and
recent.
Reading that record by hand once cost a month; an LLM agent
scanned all 1{,}200 candidate records during a coffee break.
The audit nobody could afford is now cheaper than the meeting that
discusses it.
\bnfleaf{naive\_fails}
Self-report answers badly.
The flattering account and the true account are both in the room,
and the team cannot tell them apart.
Members also underrate the skills they find easy, since routine
excellence reads, from the inside, as nothing special.
Surveys inherit both biases and add a vocabulary problem: two
members who name one skill differently count as two skills.
\bnfleaf{prior\_fails}
The trust and productivity literatures measure individuals; their
averages erase exactly the between-member differences that make a
team a team.
Bibliometric profiling comes closer, yet it counts topics rather
than strengths: it can say what a team wrote about, never what the
writing was reliably good at.
\bnfleaf{key\_idea}
Read the record instead.
A repertory grid, computed from the team's own coded papers, rates
every member on every elicited strength; sorted, the grid makes
strengths and blind spots adjacent and countable.
\bnfleaf{epigram}
{\em ``Asking a team what it is good at is the mistake; reading
what it already wrote is the fix.''}
\bnfleaf{this\_paper\_does}
This paper computes that grid for six authors.
An LLM agent fetched each author's most-cited papers, coded them
against the ICSE'27 research areas, and played triad interviewee;
constructs that agreed above 90\% were merged into the grid's
rows.
The team is then scored against the nine areas and its blind
spots priced: skills held by one member, areas served by none.
\bnfleaf{christening}
We name the instrument the FOCUS grid, after the sorting algorithm
it borrows from Shaw and Gaines, and use that name hereafter.
\bnfleaf{rq}
RQ1: Can a team's strengths be read from its corpus alone?
\bnfleaf{answer}
\textbf{Yes: on author review, the corpus grid reproduced [17 of
19] merged constructs, and the two misses were vocabulary
disputes, not new skills.}
\bnfleaf{rq}
RQ2: Does corpus measurement beat self-report?
\bnfleaf{answer}
\textbf{Yes: corpus columns matched the areas of the team's next
accepted papers at [83\%], against [61\%] for self-description,
and the corpus found both scarce skills that self-report missed.}
\bnfleaf{rq}
RQ3: Where should this team not work alone?
\bnfleaf{answer}
\textbf{Security and testing: no member sits within [78\%] of
either area, the two areas cling to each other rather than to any
person, and both are flagged for recruitment rather than
retraining.}
\bnfleaf{contrib}
a strengths instrument computed from public records (grid plus
FOCUS sort), runnable on any team in minutes, with no survey and
no interview;
\bnfleaf{contrib}
a measured map of six researchers against the nine ICSE'27 areas,
with its two blind spots priced;
\bnfleaf{contrib}
blend arithmetic showing that cross-wing topics beat every single
area for this team;
\bnfleaf{repro\_url}
a replication package: github.com/timm/how2rite.
\bnfleaf{caveat}
A corpus records what a team finished, not all it can do; hence
the grid can misprice a skill that never reached print.
We accept that bias because, for the purpose of staffing a paper,
a strength that never survives to publication is not yet a
strength.
\bnfleaf{caveat}
Citation counts favor the old; a skill acquired last year has had
no time to be cited.
The grid therefore reads two windows, all-time and the last five
years, and flags any construct that appears in only one of them.
\bnfleaf{roadmap}
The rest of this paper is structured as follows.
Section 2 describes repertory grids and the FOCUS sort.
Section 3 computes the grid for this team; Section 4 maps the team
to the field and prices its blind spots.
Section 5 reports the three experiments behind RQ1 to RQ3.
Section 6 discusses observations, threats to validity, and future
work.
Section 7 concludes with advice for teams that would repeat this
exercise on their own corpus.

\scriptsize
\bnfleaf{axes}
Shaw sorts research papers by three axes~\cite{shaw03}.
The question asked may seek a method of development, a method of
analysis, the design or evaluation of an instance, a
generalization, or a feasibility check.
The result produced may be a procedure, a qualitative model, an
empirical model, an analytic model, a tool, a solution, or a
report.
The validation offered ranges over analysis, evaluation,
experience, example, persuasion, and assertion.
Two of the axes form a grid, and the third axis grades every
cell.
The reading set of Figure~\ref{fig:lit} sorts cleanly under that
scheme.
\bnfleaf{wing}
Generalization questions with report results own the citation
knee.
Giray's review of deep defect prediction (JSS'22) and Zhang's
mapping of critical-scenario testing (TSE'22) both validate by
analysis of a coded corpus.
Each reads hundreds of papers so that its readers need not;
neither runs a new experiment.
\bnfleaf{wing}
Design-and-evaluation questions cluster in the tooling wing.
Xu's in-IDE code generation study (TOSEM'22) and Amaral's
compliance checker (TSE'22) each ship a tool validated by
evaluation.
Here the field behaves like classic software engineering; the
novelty is the assistant inside the loop.
Validation by evaluation is the scarcest coin in this reading
set, and only the tooling wing pays it.
\bnfleaf{wing}
The newest wing runs on experience.
C\^amara's report on generative AI in modeling (SoSyM'23) and
Khojah's observational study of ChatGPT use (Proc.\ ACM'24) both
study individual practitioners, validated by lived usage.
Such papers are quick to write and quick to date; they mark where
the field is looking, not yet what it knows.
\bnfleaf{where\_we\_sit}
The team's own seeds sit in that same report-and-experience cell
(Gregorio on accessibility, EMSE; Faubel on MLOps and Su on AI
agents, IEEE Software).
We already live in the crowded square, which is exactly why the
empty ones matter to us.
\bnfleaf{crowded}
Mapped so, the field shows one crowded cell.
Reports of individual usage abound, and every year adds more
voices to the same square.
\bnfleaf{empty}
Empirical models of teams are absent, and no paper here validates
a team-level claim by evaluation.
The grid does the arguing; the gap needs no rhetoric from us.
An empty cell is not a criticism of the field, rather it is an
address for the next paper.
\bnfleaf{issue}
Team-level empirical models of strength are missing; every model
in this set predicts for one developer at a time.
\bnfleaf{issue}
Evaluation-grade validation of assistant drift is missing; the
experience wing notices drift, yet nothing here measures it.
\bnfleaf{issue}
Longitudinal designs separating tool effect from team learning
are missing; a cross-section cannot say who improved, the
assistant or the team.
\bnfleaf{claimed}
The rest of this paper addresses the first two issues; Sections 3
and 4 build the team-level model, and Section 5 validates it at
evaluation grade.
\bnfleaf{deferred}
The third issue needs years of observation, hence it is left to
future work.
We flag it now so that the record shows a choice, not an
oversight.

\begin{pause}{the search}
Ask: are the goal line and the two wing queries the right words?
Is 2021 the right floor for ``recent''?
Is the venue list (see the box) the SE you mean, and are the three
coding flags the vocabulary you would use?
Is a knee that keeps \litKnee{} of \litSE{} papers too
greedy?\\[0.2em] To change the outcome: edit the query strings,
{\ttfamily VENUES}, and {\ttfamily FLAGS} in {\ttfamily litfig.py},
delete the cache in {\ttfamily tmp/}, and rerun; both figures, the
table, and every number in this section regenerate.
\end{pause}
